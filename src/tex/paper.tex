\abstract{%
  Scientific discovery and engineering problem solving rely on computational
  methods implemented in diverse software ecosystems and executed on heterogeneous
  hardware platforms by collaborators with complementary skills and toolsets.
  We introduce the \emph{computational lab}: a goal-oriented sociotechnical system
  of \emph{computational units}, \emph{data}, and \emph{human collaborators}.
  Managing heterogeneity across all three makes assembling and sustaining such a lab
  non-trivial from the outset. Without a principled approach, the lab grows
  increasingly fragile as its components evolve independently.
  We present SHOWME.how, an approach built on three complementary pillars:
  \emph{Isolation}, \emph{Interoperation}, and \emph{Orchestration}.
  Computational units are isolated with their dependencies in a self-contained
  environment; they interoperate through language-agnostic data formats and
  transfer protocols; and data movement, scheduling, and resource management
  are orchestrated using scientific workflow management systems~(SWMS).
  Together, the pillars ensure that labs tolerate updates to individual units,
  handle variability across languages and platforms, and accommodate new units
  without modifying existing ones, allowing them to sustainably grow and scale.
  We demonstrate the approach in three use cases: Bayesian parameter calibration
  in blood hemolysis and geophysical mass flow simulation, and benchmarking of
  high-dimensional Gaussian process emulators.%
}
\keywords{scientific workflows, heterogeneity, reproducibility, FAIR, computational labs,
          high-throughput computing, inverse problems, benchmarking}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% =============================================================================

Modern science and engineering are computational through and through, and
increasingly collaborative. A single research question now routinely draws on
large datasets, methods implemented in several programming languages, hardware
ranging from laptops to HPC clusters and cloud, and a team whose members bring
different expertise and different toolsets~\cite{cohenboulakia2017workflows,suter2025terminology}.
A computational engineer may work alongside a domain scientist, a specialist in
statistical inference, and a research software engineer, each most productive in
their own language, operating system, and compute environment. This diversity
is not a defect to be eliminated but an intrinsic property of how real research
teams work. The systems built from these parts tend to start working and then
grow fragile, as each component evolves on its own schedule and the connections
between them quietly break.

The FAIR principles~\cite{wilkinson2016fair,barker2022fair4rs,wilkinson2025fairworkflows,goble2020fairworkflows}
articulate what well-managed research data, software, and workflows should look
like, and the scientific workflow community has made reproducibility a central
goal~\cite{cohenboulakia2017workflows}. Yet knowing what to aim for leaves open
the harder question of how to build infrastructure that survives real
heterogeneity across data, methods, compute environments, and people at the
same time. Reproducibility is necessary but not sufficient; the broader and
largely unmanaged challenge is heterogeneity itself, which persists even when
every individual result can be reproduced.

This paper makes two contributions. First, we introduce the \emph{computational
lab} (Section~\ref{sec:complab}), a goal-oriented sociotechnical system of
computational units, data, and human collaborators, as a framing that treats
heterogeneity as the dimension to be managed rather than designed away. Second,
we present SHOWME.how (Section~\ref{sec:approach}), an approach built on three
complementary pillars, Isolation, Interoperation, and Orchestration, that
together make a lab robust enough to tolerate perturbations, handle variability,
withstand adverse conditions, and grow with new units and collaborators. We
ground the approach in three use cases spanning biomedical engineering,
geophysical hazard simulation, and high-dimensional uncertainty quantification
(Section~\ref{sec:usecases}). An open-source repository of workflow examples,
isolation templates, and patterns accompanies the paper.

\section{The Computational Lab}
\label{sec:complab}
% =============================================================================

% AC===========================================================================
We define a \emph{computational lab} as a goal-oriented sociotechnical system
whose purpose is to enable scientific discovery and to address complex
engineering challenges through computational means. Typical goals range from answering
research questions, developing new models and algorithms, and understanding
complex natural phenomena, to solving inverse problems, performing
optimisation, and benchmarking competing methods for application-driven tasks.

The computational lab is to computation-driven research what the wet lab is to
experimental science. In both settings, work proceeds through a continuous
cycle of hypothesis, experiment, observation, analysis, and conclusion, applied
by scientists pursuing discovery and by engineers solving problems.
In practice, wet labs and computational labs are not disjoint. Contemporary
wet labs increasingly rely on computational labs for data acquisition,
processing, analysis, and modelling.

A computational lab is composed of three classes of components: computational
units, data, and human collaborators. A \emph{computational unit} couples a
\emph{model} or \emph{algorithm}, in its concrete implementation, with the
\emph{compute environment} in which it runs. The compute environment has two
layers. The software ecosystem covers the programming language and operating
system required by the implementation. The hardware platform covers the
physical resources on which the computational unit executes. \emph{Data} comprises the inputs
consumed by computational units, the outputs they produce, and the intermediate
artefacts exchanged between them. \emph{Human collaborators} are the
researchers, engineers, and domain experts who design and operate the lab, and
interpret its results. In principle this role could also be filled by AI agents, but we
restrict our attention to human collaborators in this work.

The components of a computational lab are heterogeneous by nature.
Data appears in many formats and structures, from geometries, boundary
conditions, and observations as inputs, to quantities of interest,
visualisations, and reports as outputs. These data may reside on local disks,
in cloud object storage, or in community data repositories.
Models and algorithms span physics-based simulators, data-driven surrogates,
and frameworks for optimisation, inference, and analysis, each typically tied
to a particular language. Compute environments vary in both software ecosystem and hardware platform.
The software ecosystem ranges across programming languages such as Python,
Julia, R, C++, Fortran, and MATLAB, among others, and operating systems such as Linux,
macOS, and Windows. The hardware platform ranges across local workstations,
HPC clusters, and cloud, with accelerators such as GPUs alongside CPUs. This diversity is not incidental but a
direct consequence of the human collaborators themselves: domain specialists,
methods experts, data scientists, and research software engineers each bring
different skills, training, and preferred toolsets to the lab. A computational lab must therefore accommodate the tools and environments its
collaborators bring, rather than impose a single one.

The notion of a computational lab is distinct from several related terms. A
\emph{scientific workflow} captures the pipeline of computational steps but
treats neither heterogeneity nor human collaborators as first-class concerns.
A \emph{digital laboratory} (or \emph{e-lab}) usually denotes a domain-specific
platform built around LIMS or ELN systems for experimental data management.
The term \emph{computational laboratory} appears occasionally in the literature
but is used colloquially, without a formal definition.

Figure~\ref{fig:heterogeneous} illustrates these components in a representative
computational lab: the Bayesian parameter calibration of a shallow mass flow
model used in geophysical hazard simulation.
This inverse problem is solved by chaining three computational units: a
forward simulator, a surrogate emulator, and a calibration process, each
developed and operated by collaborators with complementary expertise. How these components are made
to work together is the subject of Section~\ref{sec:approach}.

\subsection{Recurring Patterns}
\label{sec:patterns}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------

% AC===========================================================================
Three patterns recur across computational labs in science and engineering
(Figure~\ref{fig:lab_patterns}); we introduce them here as they are
referenced throughout the paper.

\paragraph{Pattern A: Queue-Based Data Pipeline}
A \texttt{Generator} produces a large set of inputs (parameter sweeps,
different initial conditions, or sampled configurations); each generated
input is independently dispatched to a \texttt{Method} (a model or algorithm).
The \texttt{Method} processes each input and produces an output; the
outputs are collected by a \texttt{Collector} and aggregated into a
dataset for downstream use.
Common instances include training a surrogate emulator from a physics-based
simulator using a space-filling sampling scheme (see Figure~\ref{fig:heterogeneous}),
forward uncertainty propagation via Monte Carlo sampling where output statistics
are derived from the ensemble, and Sobol sensitivity analysis where the full
(input, output) dataset is processed to compute variance-based sensitivity indices.

\paragraph{Pattern B: Fan-Out/Fan-In}
A \texttt{Generator} produces a shared input that is dispatched to multiple
\texttt{Method} implementations; each produces an output, and the outputs
are gathered by a \texttt{Collector} for comparison.
Herein, the \texttt{Method} implementations are of the same model or algorithm,
differing in their mathematical formulation, programming language, or both.
Common instances include method benchmarking, where competing implementations
are evaluated against a shared input; ensemble runs with different random seeds,
whose outputs are aggregated to characterise variability or assess convergence;
and inference diagnostics, where shared inference results are fanned out to
complementary analyses and gathered into a report.

\paragraph{Pattern C: Model-Based $X$ in an Online Loop}
A \texttt{Model} and an \texttt{Algorithm} are coupled at run time as a
client--server pair; the \texttt{Algorithm} drives the \texttt{Model}
iteratively, requiring both to run simultaneously.
Common instances include inverse uncertainty quantification, such as parameter
calibration, model selection, and data selection, and optimisation, where
the model is queried iteratively to infer properties or search for optimal
inputs, and data assimilation, where the model state is updated sequentially
as new observations arrive.

\medskip
In all three patterns, a computational unit may itself house tightly coupled
internal components, such as an MPI-parallel multi-physics solver or a
co-simulation.

\subsection{Robustness}
\label{sec:robustness_def}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------

% AC===========================================================================
Productive science and engineering requires that methods be reusable and
that new work can build on existing ones; to satisfy this, a computational
lab must keep running correctly as its data, methods, dependencies,
collaborators, and infrastructure change. Drawing on the general engineering
sense of the word, we say that a computational lab is \emph{robust} if it
satisfies four properties:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Tolerate perturbations}: remain functional when individual
        computational units are updated or replaced.
  \item \textbf{Handle variability}: remain effective across diverse operating
        systems, programming languages, hardware platforms, and data formats.
  \item \textbf{Withstand adverse conditions}: remain operational under
        infrastructure migration and collaborator changes, and recoverable on
        interruption of a long-running computation.
  \item \textbf{Support modular extensibility}: accommodate new computational
        units and new collaborators added after the lab is first assembled,
        without breaking existing ones.
\end{enumerate}


The fourth is added because computational labs are by design constantly
evolving: they grow as new scientific questions are asked and new methods
become available. A robustness framing that does not account for this growth
is insufficient for the research setting.

\section{Challenges in Building Computational Labs}
\label{sec:challenges}
% =============================================================================

% AC===========================================================================
A computational lab is constantly evolving: computational units are updated or
replaced, new data formats emerge, collaborators join or leave, new computing
infrastructure becomes available, and problem sizes grow. Each such event is a
potential point of failure. In practice, the approaches that teams use to manage
heterogeneity fall short when the lab evolves.

\subsection{Current Approaches and Their Limitations}
\label{sec:challenges:approaches}

\paragraph{Data feedforward.}
Collaborators complete their part of the computation and manually transfer results
to the next collaborator via shared drives or messaging. This preserves each
collaborator's environment, at the cost of data versioning, traceability, and provenance.
For labs following Pattern~C (Section~\ref{sec:patterns}), such as Bayesian
parameter calibration, where a UQ method must query a forward model iteratively
throughout the inference process, the approach is not merely inefficient but
physically infeasible.

\paragraph{Software wrappers.}
An alternative is to write wrapper libraries that expose models and algorithms
implemented in different programming languages through a unified interface,
allowing all computational units to run in a single shared environment.
While this enables in-memory data passing, it ties the computational unit to
the wrapper's implementation: when the underlying library updates, the wrapper
must follow. Such wrappers frequently go stale, either breaking the lab or
locking it to an older, suboptimal version of the model or algorithm.
Moreover, adopting a newly published model or algorithm in a different
programming language requires a new wrapper or forgoing it entirely.

The limitations of the above two approaches reveal deeper technical challenges
that persist regardless of which is adopted.

\subsection{Technical Challenges}
\label{sec:challenges:challenges}

\subsubsection*{Computational Units}

\paragraph{Unit-level reproducibility.}
A reproducible computational lab requires that each computational unit be
individually reproducible. In practice, missing or incomplete environment
specifications often make it impossible to rebuild the software environment
in which a unit was intended to run. Workflow-level reproducibility additionally
requires explicit pinning of RNG seeds, operating system behaviour, and parallel
execution ordering.

\paragraph{Dependency conflicts.}
Computational units developed independently sometimes require incompatible versions of
the same library or runtime. Sharing a single environment, as in the software wrapper approach, makes these
conflicts irreconcilable, forcing collaborators to compromise on versions or
maintain fragile patches.

\paragraph{Environment drift.}
Even without deliberate changes, a computational unit that ran correctly months ago
may silently fail today, as upstream packages are updated, system libraries change,
and package registries remove older versions. Without explicit environment pinning,
the software ecosystem drifts over time and results become irreproducible.

\subsubsection*{Data}

\paragraph{Language-tied data formats.}
Data is commonly serialised in formats tied to a specific language, for example
\texttt{.pkl} or \texttt{.npy} in Python, or \texttt{.jld2} in Julia. These are
convenient within one language ecosystem but become barriers when a new collaborator
or method works in a different language, forcing data conversion or language
adoption. Adopting a newly published model or algorithm in a different language compounds
the problem, as it either cannot read existing data or requires a conversion
step, adding friction precisely where rapid prototyping matters most.

\paragraph{Provenance and traceability.}
As data flows between computational units, tracking which version of which unit
produced a given result becomes non-trivial. Without explicit provenance,
debugging unexpected results requires reconstructing the chain of computation
by hand, which is expensive when units involve
long-running HPC jobs. More broadly, without provenance, scientific claims
produced by the lab cannot be verified by reviewers or collaborators.

\subsubsection*{Compute Environment}

\paragraph{Infrastructure portability.}
Moving a computational lab from a local workstation to an HPC cluster or cloud
environment surfaces assumptions baked into computational units about the host
environment: container runtimes differ, schedulers vary, and what runs on a laptop
may not run on HPC without unit-code changes. A further complication arises when
different units must run on different infrastructure simultaneously, such as a
computationally expensive model on HPC alongside a lighter unit running locally,
requiring the lab to coordinate across infrastructure boundaries at runtime.

\paragraph{Coordination overhead.}
As a computational lab grows in the number of units and the complexity of their
dependencies, manually tracking which units to run, in what order, and where their
outputs go becomes error-prone and unsustainable. Interrupted runs compound the problem, 
requiring manual identification of completed steps and selective re-execution. 
Such a process is both fragile and time-consuming.

\medskip
What we need is not more tools, but a principled approach that addresses these
challenges at their root and makes computational labs robust as they grow.
SHOWME.how is such an approach.

\section{SHOWME.how}
\label{sec:approach}
% =============================================================================

The SHOWME.how approach is built on three complementary pillars: Isolation,
Interoperation, and Orchestration (Figure~\ref{fig:three_pillars}). Each pillar
addresses a distinct class of challenges identified in
Section~\ref{sec:challenges}. A lab can adopt any one of the pillars independently and already gain
robustness along that dimension, adding the others as the lab grows and its
requirements evolve.

\begin{figure}[!ht]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{figures/showmehow_concept.pdf}
  \caption{Overview of the SHOWME.how approach: Isolation, Interoperation, and
           Orchestration, with representative technologies for each pillar.}
  \label{fig:three_pillars}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Isolation}
\label{sec:isolation}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------

The goal of Isolation is that any computational unit can participate in the lab
regardless of its programming language, operating system, and dependencies,
without interfering with any other unit. We can achieve this by encapsulating the
compute environment of each unit through package management and containerisation,
so that units remain self-contained and portable.

The right isolation strategy depends on the specific requirements of the lab.
The following questions help identify it:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item What software ecosystem does each unit require, in terms of
        programming language, operating system, and dependencies?
  \item Where will each unit run? On a collaborator's laptop, an HPC cluster,
        or a cloud environment?
  \item What level of reproducibility is required? Is numerical equivalence
        across platforms sufficient, or is a fully controlled environment that
        also pins system libraries and floating-point behaviour needed?
  \item How heterogeneous is the lab in terms of collaborators, operating
        systems, CPU architectures, and infrastructure, and how much is it
        expected to change?
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Iso1 --- Package management.}
Isolation at this level is achieved using a cross-platform, language-agnostic
package manager. We recommend conda, mamba, or micromamba~\cite{conda,mamba},
as they are natively supported by the SWMSs we discuss in
Section~\ref{sec:orchestration}. A unit's software dependencies are specified
in an \texttt{environment.yml} file, but this alone does not guarantee
reproducibility: package versions and all their indirect dependencies are not
pinned, and some packages resolve differently across operating systems and CPU
architectures. We therefore generate \emph{conda-lock files}, one per operating
system and architecture, which pin every package to an exact version so that
the software dependencies of a unit can be recreated identically on any
matching platform. Per-OS, per-architecture locks are sufficient for numerically equivalent
reproducibility, ensuring consistent results across the platforms each
collaborator uses. Other package managers such as pixi~\cite{pixi} and the Julia
package manager~\cite{juliapkg} have locking built in by default, but are not
yet natively supported by the SWMSs we consider.

\paragraph{Iso2 --- Containerisation.}
When package managers with pinned lock files are not sufficient — for units
that rely on legacy software, C++ or Fortran codebases with complex build
systems, or dependencies not available on conda — containerisation is the next
step. A container packages not just the software dependencies but the entire
operating system layer, so that the unit is fully independent of the host
operating system. Each unit requires a \texttt{Dockerfile} as its build definition, which can then be built into a container image using Docker,
Podman, or any compatible container runtime. We recommend one container image per unit, so that a dependency change in one
unit cannot affect any other. Container images are architecture-specific; when the lab spans multiple CPU
architectures, we need to build multi-architecture manifests that bundle
architecture-specific images under a single tag, allowing the correct variant
to be pulled automatically.\footnote{\texttt{docker buildx} is a Docker CLI
plugin that enables building images for multiple CPU architectures from a
single command, producing a multi-architecture manifest.}

\paragraph{Iso3 --- HPC.}
A Docker image is sufficient for laptop and cloud environments. HPC clusters
typically restrict Docker for security reasons, making
Apptainer~\cite{apptainer,kurtzer2017singularity} the container runtime of
choice. Apptainer can build images directly from an
Apptainer definition file, but when the build definition requires frequent
iteration, the lack of layer-based caching makes development slow, as every change
triggers a full rebuild. We therefore recommend
the pipeline \texttt{Dockerfile} $\to$ Docker image $\to$ Apptainer
\texttt{.sif}, converting the Docker image to an Apptainer image as a final
step. This keeps a single build definition that benefits from Docker's layer
caching during development, while producing an Apptainer image ready for HPC
deployment.


\paragraph{Iso4 --- Automation.}
Maintaining lock files across operating systems and architectures, and building
container images across CPU architectures, is combinatorial work that does not
scale when done manually. We recommend automating this process using continuous
integration (CI). A matrix build can regenerate per-OS, per-architecture conda-lock
files whenever the \texttt{environment.yml} changes, and build and publish
multi-architecture container images whenever the \texttt{Dockerfile} changes.
This shifts the maintenance burden from individual collaborators to a single
automated pipeline.

For units where conda-lock alone is insufficient but a full Dockerfile is too
heavy, a middle ground exists, achieved by wrapping a locked conda environment inside
a minimal container image. This combines conda's resolution of language-level
dependencies with the container's control over the system layer, in a single
artefact that can be built and automated in the same CI pipeline.

\subsection{Interoperation}
\label{sec:interoperation}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------

The goal of Interoperation is that data can flow between computational units
regardless of the programming language each unit uses or the infrastructure on
which it runs. We can achieve this by adopting language-agnostic data interchange
formats and platform-agnostic data exchange protocols at unit boundaries. This directly addresses the Interoperability dimension of
FAIR~\cite{wilkinson2016fair}, which calls for data to use broadly applicable
language for knowledge representation, here realised through concrete format
and protocol choices at unit boundaries.

The right interoperation strategy depends on the specific requirements of the
lab. The following questions help identify it:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item What is the nature of the data being exchanged? Is it tabular,
        hierarchical, or multi-dimensional arrays?
  \item Do all units run locally, or does the lab span multiple compute
        locations such as a laptop, an HPC cluster, or cloud infrastructure?
  \item What is the pattern of data exchange? Does the lab transfer data once
        per unit, or does it involve repeated exchanges during execution?
  \item How might the interoperation needs of the lab grow? Will the volume
        of data increase, or will the heterogeneity of units exchanging it?
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Int1 --- Data formats.}
All data at unit boundaries must use language-agnostic interchange formats,
so that adding a unit in a new programming language never requires changes to
existing units. Language-specific binary formats such as \texttt{.pkl}
(Python), \texttt{.npy} (NumPy/Python), and \texttt{.jld2} (Julia) should not
appear at boundaries, as a collaborator using R or C++ would need to install
and maintain a Python or Julia runtime solely to read another unit's output.
Text formats such as CSV and JSON are portable and an acceptable starting point
when the data is not numerically heavy; they become a poor choice when the data
consists primarily of floating-point values, as they are string-based, which
introduces significant storage overhead and risks precision loss when
insufficient digits are written.\footnote{A 64-bit double requires 17
significant decimal digits for a guaranteed round-trip, leading to string
representations of 20--24 ASCII characters (160--192 bits) versus 64 bits in
binary (a bloat factor of approximately 2.5--3$\times$).} For data consisting
primarily of floating-point values, we recommend binary formats: HDF5~\cite{hdf5}
or netCDF for hierarchical and structured data, Zarr~\cite{moore2023zarr} for
multi-dimensional arrays, and Apache Arrow/Parquet~\cite{apachearrow} for
tabular data.

\paragraph{Int2 --- Location.}
The exchange mechanism depends on where the units execute. Filesystem exchange is the
simplest starting point, with no network dependency; each
unit writes its output to a file path and the next unit reads from it.
This holds whether units execute on a single laptop or across nodes of an HPC
cluster with a shared filesystem such as Lustre or GPFS. On Windows and macOS,
Docker containers run inside a Linux virtual machine and cannot directly access
the host's inter-process communication mechanisms; filesystem exchange remains
the only reliable option for same-machine exchange between a containerised unit
and a non-containerised one. We recommend starting with filesystem exchange and
switching to remote storage
only when the lab spans separate infrastructures with no shared
filesystem between them, such as a laptop and a cloud instance or two separate
HPC systems. S3-compatible object storage suits this case well, as
it is accessible from any infrastructure via HTTP and requires no shared mount.
When the source or destination exposes a filesystem rather than an object store,
such as a remote workstation or an existing dataset on a collaborator's server,
SFTP is more appropriate.

\paragraph{Int3 --- Throughput.}
Throughput governs how much data can flow between units per unit time. It
becomes the limiting factor in two situations: when units exchange large volumes
of data in bulk over a network; and when units exchange data at high frequency,
as in Pattern~C (Section~\ref{sec:patterns}), where one unit queries another
repeatedly during execution. For bulk transfers, we recommend
Zarr~\cite{moore2023zarr} over HDF5; unlike HDF5, Zarr is designed for chunked
access over HTTP and S3, making it well-suited to large array data moving across
a network or object storage. For high-frequency exchange, we recommend an
HTTP-based client--server approach, where one unit acts as a server exposing
its computation and the other acts as a client that queries it repeatedly. The
approach requires choosing a transport protocol and a serialization format. HTTP
provides a simple, cross-language point-to-point interface with broad support.
For the serialization format, JSON is acceptable when payloads are small, but
its overhead grows with array size and can become a bottleneck for large
numerical payloads; binary formats such as MessagePack or Protocol Buffers are
significantly faster. UMBridge~\cite{seelinger2023umbridge} provides a
ready-made implementation of this approach for model-based uncertainty
quantification; it uses HTTP and JSON and requires no manual data transfer, but
its interface accepts only array-based inputs and outputs, making it unsuitable
when units need to exchange data that is not array-based.

As a rule of thumb, start with CSV or JSON and filesystem exchange; move to
binary formats such as HDF5, Zarr, or Arrow/Parquet as the data becomes
primarily floating-point. For the exchange mechanism, switch to remote storage
when the lab spans separate infrastructures; adopt an HTTP-based client--server
approach only when units need to exchange data at high frequency during
execution.


% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Orchestration}
\label{sec:orchestration}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------

The goal of Orchestration is that the lab runs reliably end-to-end, even as
units are added, collaborators change, or the infrastructure it runs on evolves.
We can achieve this by using a SWMS to automate
scheduling, data movement, and checkpointing, so the lab can operate
seamlessly as its needs grow.

The right orchestration strategy depends on the specific requirements of the
lab. The following questions help identify it:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Is the lab complex enough to warrant a SWMS? How many units
        are there, and how complex are their dependencies?
  \item Does the lab need to execute across different infrastructure, such as
        running some units locally and others on an HPC cluster or cloud, and
        what schedulers or executors does that require?
  \item Would restarting the lab from scratch on failure be costly, either
        because individual units are long-running or because the lab involves
        a large number of unit executions?
  \item Does the lab require any unit to run as a long-lived service process,
        as in Pattern~C, rather than completing after a single execution?
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Orch1 --- Automation.}
A lab that runs once, with few units and no need for cross-infrastructure
execution, can be coordinated with a script. A SWMS becomes
necessary when the lab needs to run repeatedly, when units span different
environments or infrastructure, or when manual data movement and environment
activation becomes a bottleneck.

We use Nextflow~\cite{ditommaso2017nextflow} and
Snakemake~\cite{molder2021snakemake} as representative examples throughout
this section; SHOWME.how is not tied to any particular manager, and a broader
survey of the landscape is available in Suter et al.~\cite{suter2025terminology}
and Dierckx et al.~\cite{dierckx2022evaluation}. Nextflow runs on Linux and
macOS (with WSL on Windows); Snakemake is cross-platform. Workflow extensions
in Nextflow require Groovy and the JVM; Snakemake extensions are written in
Python. Both managers share the same unit contract,
where each unit declares its inputs, outputs, script, and compute
environment.\footnote{Snakemake's native container runtime is Apptainer; Docker
images declared in the environment are converted automatically.} This contract
ties directly to both preceding pillars; the environment declaration activates
the isolation strategy defined for each unit, and the input/output declarations
specify where and how data crosses unit boundaries.

\paragraph{Orch2 --- Scheduling.}
Scheduling determines where and how units execute. Both Nextflow and Snakemake
follow a static planning approach~\cite{suter2025terminology}, resolving the
full dependency graph before execution begins and submitting each unit only
when its declared inputs are available. They differ in how dependencies are
expressed; Nextflow uses a dataflow paradigm where units are connected by
explicit data channels, and Snakemake uses a file-based paradigm where
dependencies are inferred from input and output file paths. A SWMS
submits each unit to the appropriate executor automatically, whether a local
process on a laptop, a SLURM job on an HPC cluster, or a cloud executor. In most cases, moving the lab to a different
infrastructure requires no changes
to unit code; only the configuration specifying where and how units execute
changes.

\paragraph{Orch3 --- Incremental execution.}
Incremental execution means the SWMS tracks which units have
completed successfully and skips them on rerun, so a failure mid-run restarts
from the last completed unit rather than from scratch. This becomes essential when individual units are long-running or the lab must
execute a large number of them.  Snakemake tracks completion via output
file timestamps and checksums; a unit is considered done if its output exists
and is newer than its inputs. This can be fooled by manually copying or
touching output files, and changing the unit code does not automatically
trigger a rerun. Nextflow uses a work directory with per-task hashes derived
from inputs and script content, so script changes do trigger reruns
automatically; the work directory accumulates heavily over time and requires
periodic cleanup with \texttt{nextflow clean}, and the \texttt{--resume} flag
requires the work directory to still exist. Neither manager tracks changes to
external dependencies outside the declared environment, and incremental
execution assumes deterministic outputs. Both managers also capture basic
execution provenance. Snakemake generates
an HTML report with DAG visualisation, runtime statistics, and input/output
records; Nextflow supports provenance through the \texttt{nf-prov} plugin,
which produces records in RO-Crate or PROV format but requires explicit
setup.

\paragraph{Orch4 --- Service processes.}
Pattern~C (Section~\ref{sec:patterns}) requires a unit to run as a long-lived
service process, specifically a model server that must remain alive while a
client unit queries it repeatedly. Scientific SWMSs assume each
unit starts, runs, and exits; a service process breaks this assumption. In
Snakemake, a unit can be declared as a service rule; it is started before
dependent units and terminated automatically once all consumers have finished.
Nextflow has no native support for service processes. The common
workaround is a file-based sentinel, where the server writes a file when it is
ready and the client unit declares that file as an input dependency. The
workaround has two failure modes. If the server crashes before writing the
sentinel, the client unit never starts and the workflow stalls; if the server
crashes after the client has started, the client cannot reach the server's URL
and port and crashes with a connection error. Labs using Snakemake can use
native service rules; labs using Nextflow can use the sentinel workaround.

\subsection{Bayesian Calibration of a Hemolysis Model}
\label{sec:usecase:hemolysis}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------

Blood hemolysis models predict red blood cell damage under mechanical stress and
are used to evaluate the safety of ventricular assist devices. The goal of this
lab is to infer the parameters of a hemolysis model from experimental
measurements via Bayesian parameter calibration. The lab instantiates
Patterns~B and~C jointly. Each calibration couples an MCMC process to the forward
model in an online client--server loop, requiring both to run simultaneously
(Pattern~C). The lab fans this calibration out across a sweep of priors, hemolysis
models, and datasets that run in parallel, and a collection step organises the
per-run results into a single structured output directory for comparison across
the sweep (Pattern~B).
Three collaborators with complementary expertise contribute to the lab: a
domain expert operating on macOS in Python, a UQ-methods expert on Windows in
R and Python, and an RSE on Linux working across multiple languages. The RSE
ported the domain expert's Python forward model to Julia, yielding a
10--100$\times$ speedup over the NumPy baseline; both implementations coexist
as alternatives selectable at runtime.

Each computational unit runs in its own Docker container (Iso2), with one image
per unit so that a dependency change in one cannot affect any other. The same
Dockerfile drives both Docker images for local development and Apptainer images
for HPC deployment (Iso3), so that moving the lab between infrastructure requires
no changes to unit code. Field data is downloaded from a remote repository and
preprocessed to CSV; MCMC outputs are written in NetCDF format compatible with
standard Bayesian diagnostics tooling, so downstream analysis units can consume
results regardless of language (Int1). The forward model server and MCMC client
communicate via a filesystem sentinel: the server writes a readiness flag to a
shared directory, and the MCMC process declares that file as an input dependency
in Nextflow, so the calibration starts only once the server is confirmed
ready (Orch4).

The lab has been ported from laptop to HPC by changing only the Nextflow executor
profile, switching the job scheduler and converting Docker images to Apptainer;
no unit code was modified. Diagnostic units were added after the lab was first
assembled without modifying any existing unit, demonstrating modular
extensibility. The lab currently operates within a stable collaborative environment shared by
all three contributors; conda-lock pinning and CI automation (Iso1, Iso4) are
the next engineering steps before wider distribution. The lab is available at
\url{https://github.com/nicodirkes/bpc_hemolysis}.

\subsection{Bayesian Calibration of a Mass Flow Model}
\label{sec:usecase:massflow}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------

Geophysical mass flow simulators model natural hazards such as landslides and
avalanches. Running the forward simulator is computationally expensive; Bayesian
calibration therefore proceeds in two stages. First, a Latin Hypercube Sampling
process generates a parameter design, the forward simulator evaluates each
configuration on HPC, and the responses are collected to train a Gaussian process
surrogate (Pattern~A). Second, the trained surrogate is coupled to a Markov
chain Monte Carlo process for parameter inference, with the surrogate serving
predictions on demand throughout the run (Pattern~C). The four computational
units span four language ecosystems: Latin Hypercube Sampling in Julia, the
physics-based forward simulator in Python, surrogate training and serving in R,
and MCMC inference in Python.

Each unit is packaged in a dedicated Apptainer container (Iso2, Iso3), providing
fully isolated environments and enabling HPC deployment without modifying unit
code. Latin Hypercube samples and forward model responses are exchanged as CSV
files; MCMC output arrays are written in HDF5 format (Int1). The surrogate unit is
decomposed into a training process and a serving process so that the emulator
need not be retrained on every run; the trained model is exchanged between these
two sub-processes as an RDS file, which is appropriate here because both
sub-processes share an R environment and no language crossing occurs at that seam.
The serving process exposes the surrogate over HTTP following the UMBridge
model-server interface~\cite{seelinger2023umbridge}, implemented in R using
httpuv and jsonlite;\footnote{The R server implementation of the UMBridge
interface is an ongoing contribution to the UMBridge project.} the Python MCMC
client queries it over HTTP without any language bridging, applying the
client--server approach from Int3.

The lab runs end-to-end in Nextflow with all Apptainer containers built per
unit. % TODO: add one sentence of concrete robustness evidence (what was
% ported, what was verified — e.g., HPC run, post-hoc unit addition, etc.)
The lab is available at \url{https://github.com/thealanjason/bpc_massflow}.

\subsection{Benchmarking of Gaussian Process Emulators}
\label{sec:usecase:gpe}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------

High-dimensional uncertainty quantification requires surrogate emulators that
scale to large input and output spaces. This lab benchmarks a range of Gaussian
process emulator implementations against shared datasets to provide
reproducible, language-agnostic comparisons for the UQ methods community. It is
an instance of Pattern~B: a shared dataset is fanned out to multiple emulator
implementations in parallel, each producing results independently, and the
outputs are gathered and scored by a metrics aggregation unit. The emulator
implementations span two language ecosystems: ExactGP and DKL via
PyTorch/GPyTorch in Python, and RGaSP, PCA-RGaSP, PPGaSP, BiGP, and MTGP via
RobustGaSP in R, with autoencoder and variational autoencoder variants also in
Python.

Each emulator runs in its own conda environment with a pinned lock file (Iso1),
so that adding a new emulator implementation introduces no dependency conflict
with any other. Data at all unit boundaries uses HDF5, so any emulator,
regardless of language, reads and writes through the same format without
conversion (Int1). Five benchmark datasets are included, spanning synthetic
functions and real-world phenomena including a rock avalanche, a synthetic
landslide, and a tsunami simulation; raw data is fetched from Zenodo and Figshare
at setup time and standardised to HDF5 by a shared preprocessing unit. The lab
is implemented in both Nextflow and Snakemake, demonstrating that the
Orchestration pillar is not tied to any particular SWMS; both versions support
local and SLURM execution profiles (Orch2), and the Snakemake version uses
file-based incremental execution to skip completed emulator runs on rerun
(Orch3). GPU-accelerated execution is supported for PyTorch-based emulators in
the SLURM profile.

CI is fully automated: conda lock files are regenerated whenever an environment
specification changes (Iso4), and a benchmark run is triggered on every push to
the main branch, with results uploaded as a GitHub artifact for inspection. The
lab is available in two versions: Nextflow at
\url{https://github.com/gary8564/gpe_bench_nxf} and Snakemake at
\url{https://github.com/gary8564/gpe_bench_smk}.

\section{Robustness}
\label{sec:robustness}
% =============================================================================


% AC===========================================================================
The three pillars of SHOWME.how were designed to address the failure modes of
Section~\ref{sec:challenges} directly. By doing so, each primarily secures one
or more robustness properties from Section~\ref{sec:robustness_def}: Isolation
secures the ability to tolerate perturbations; Interoperation secures the
ability to handle variability across languages and platforms; Orchestration
secures the ability to withstand adverse conditions and to support modular
extensibility, jointly covering all four. The following criteria make that
coverage explicit and checkable against a concrete lab.

\begin{table}[htb]
  \centering
  \small
  \caption{Robustness checklist for a computational lab. A lab satisfying all
           criteria can absorb change at every level without requiring
           coordinated updates across its units.}
  \label{tab:robustness}
  \begin{tabular}{p{2.4cm}p{7.2cm}p{1.8cm}}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Property} & \textbf{Criterion} & \textbf{Approach} \\
    \midrule
    \textbf{Tolerate perturbations}
      & Per-OS, per-architecture lock file or container image per unit.
      & Iso1--Iso2 \\
      & All random state seeded per thread, rank, and library.$^\dagger$
      & --- \\
      & Checkpointing enabled in the workflow manager.
      & Orch3 \\
    \midrule
    \textbf{Handle variability}
      & No language-tied serialisation format at any unit boundary.
      & Int1 \\
      & Workflow runs without unit-code changes on more than one operating system.
      & Iso1--Iso3 \\
      & Live-loop coupling (Pattern~C, Section~\ref{sec:patterns}) uses a
        language-agnostic protocol.
      & Int3 \\
    \midrule
    \textbf{Withstand adverse conditions}
      & A single \texttt{Dockerfile} drives both Docker and Apptainer images;
        porting to HPC requires no unit-code changes.
      & Iso3 \\
      & Executor configuration handles scheduler and runtime differences.
      & Orch2 \\
      & Multi-architecture manifests and CI-automated builds kept current.
      & Iso2, Iso4 \\
    \midrule
    \textbf{Support modular extensibility}
      & Each unit declares inputs, outputs, script, and environment as a
        self-contained contract.
      & Orch1 \\
      & No unit bridges programming languages through wrapper code.
      & Iso1--Iso2, Int1 \\
      & Adding a unit requires no edits to and no revalidation of existing units.
      & Orch1, Iso1--Iso2, Int1 \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}

  \smallskip
  \raggedright\footnotesize{$^\dagger$~A single global seed is insufficient
  under parallel execution; each thread, rank, and library maintaining
  independent state must be seeded separately (e.g.\ NumPy's
  \texttt{Generator}, Julia's \texttt{Random.seed!}, R's \texttt{set.seed}
  each manage independent state).}
\end{table}

This rubric is prescriptive: it states what a robust lab must satisfy, not
what any particular lab has been shown to satisfy. The hemolysis use case
(Section~\ref{sec:usecases}) satisfies several criteria by direct observation:
the lab runs end-to-end without unit-code changes on Linux, macOS, and
Windows, and diagnostic units were added post-hoc without modifying existing
ones. All boundary files use HDF5 or CSV, and a new collaborator reproduced
the lab with a \texttt{git clone}, a single conda environment for the workflow
manager, and one run command. Numerical equivalence of outputs across operating systems has
not been verified systematically; the lab involves stochastic units, and
cross-OS equivalence is contingent on the random-state seeding criterion
being fully implemented. That verification remains open.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
% =============================================================================

\paragraph{Motivating frameworks.}
The FAIR principles~\cite{wilkinson2016fair,barker2022fair4rs,goble2020fairworkflows,wilkinson2025fairworkflows}
define normative criteria for making research outputs Findable, Accessible,
Interoperable, and Reusable. SHOWME.how is a concrete implementation strategy for the Interoperability and
Reusability dimensions of FAIR. The Interoperation pillar addresses the FAIR
Interoperability dimension by requiring data at unit boundaries to use broadly
applicable knowledge representation formats
(Section~\ref{sec:interoperation}). Isolation supports the FAIR4RS Reusability
dimension~\cite{barker2022fair4rs} by ensuring that the software environment of
each unit is fully specified and reconstructible
(Section~\ref{sec:isolation}). Taken together, all three pillars enable
reusability of the computational lab as a whole: a collaborator can take over,
extend, or reproduce the lab without manual environment reconstruction, format
negotiation, or infrastructure rework.
Cohen-Boulakia et al.~\cite{cohenboulakia2017workflows} define four levels of
reproducibility for scientific workflows (repeat, replicate, reproduce, reuse)
and evaluate representative SWMSs against reproducibility-friendly criteria.
Their system-wide packaging criterion, which most evaluated systems failed to
meet, directly motivated SHOWME.how's Isolation pillar; SHOWME.how applies
environment packaging per unit rather than system-wide, so that dependency
conflicts between units are structurally prevented. They further identify
automatic dependency maintenance as an open challenge: no evaluated system
could update a broken environment without manual intervention. SHOWME.how's
Iso4 addresses this by automating lock file regeneration in CI whenever a
unit's environment specification changes. Their analysis also notes that
connecting workflows across different systems generally requires high technical
skill; SHOWME.how sidesteps this by working at unit boundaries through
language-agnostic formats, rather than requiring SWMS-level interoperability.
Their analysis addresses life-science pipelines in which units share a common
runtime; SHOWME.how extends to the setting where units are heterogeneous in
language, operating system, and infrastructure simultaneously.

\paragraph{Scientific workflow management systems.}
Nextflow~\cite{ditommaso2017nextflow} and Snakemake~\cite{molder2021snakemake}
are the SWMSs used throughout this paper as representative examples; SHOWME.how
is not tied to either. Suter et al.~\cite{suter2025terminology} provide a
unified terminology for SWMSs, distinguishing scheduling models, environment
integration, and data handling as key axes of variation across systems.
Leser et al.~\cite{leser2026workflowsystems} decompose modern SWMS into three
component classes: containers or virtual machines for portability, distributed
file systems for data exchange, and workflow engines paired with resource managers
for execution.
SHOWME.how's three pillars map directly onto these component classes: Isolation
addresses environment portability, Interoperation addresses data exchange at unit
boundaries, and Orchestration addresses workflow execution and scheduling. Dierckx et al.~\cite{dierckx2022evaluation} evaluate six SWMSs against
reproducibility and reuse criteria, finding Nextflow and Snakemake best suited
for portable workflows through per-process environment specification, the
model SHOWME.how's Isolation and Orchestration pillars build on. They also
identify a tradeoff between strongly-typed process interfaces and wrapper
overhead; SHOWME.how resolves this by requiring language-agnostic data formats
at unit boundaries rather than typed wrappers, so unit code requires no
modification.

\paragraph{Alternative environment management.}
Spack~\cite{gamblin2015spack} is an HPC-focused package manager providing
source-based builds with fine-grained control over compiler flags and
dependencies; it is natively supported by Nextflow as an alternative to conda.
Nix and Guix~\cite{courtes2015guix} provide bit-identical reproducible builds
through cryptographic hashing of the full dependency graph, offering stronger
guarantees than conda-lock. The tradeoff is adoption friction: neither is
natively integrated into Nextflow or Snakemake, so using either requires
additional tooling or custom wrapper scripts outside the standard SWMS
environment directives. SHOWME.how recommends conda-lock because it provides
numerically-equivalent reproducibility within the toolchain the SWMSs already
support, lowering the barrier to adoption. Computational labs with stricter
requirements, such as bit-identical builds or environments where upstream
package registries cannot be trusted, can consider Nix or Guix, though neither
integrates natively with SWMS environment directives and requires custom setup
beyond what is described here.

\paragraph{Complementary standards.}
CWL~\cite{amstutz2016cwl} provides a vendor-neutral standard for portable
workflow definitions. It specifies \emph{what} to run and how steps connect,
but does not prescribe how units should be isolated from one another, how data
should be serialised at unit boundaries, or how Pattern~C
(Section~\ref{sec:patterns}) should be handled when a unit must run as a
long-lived service process. SHOWME.how's three pillars address these gaps. Because a SHOWME.how lab already makes explicit the unit contracts, namely
inputs, outputs, environment, and script,\footnote{The mapping onto CWL is
direct rather than identical. A unit's inputs and outputs correspond to a
\texttt{CommandLineTool}'s \texttt{inputs} and \texttt{outputs}, its script to
\texttt{baseCommand} and \texttt{arguments}, and its environment to a
\texttt{DockerRequirement} (or the lighter-weight \texttt{SoftwareRequirement}
where containerisation is not used); CWL further
distinguishes \texttt{CommandLineTool} documents from \texttt{Workflow}
documents that wire steps together.} that CWL requires to describe a
workflow, CWL description is a natural path toward cross-system portability
and sharing via WorkflowHub~\cite{goble2021workflowhub}. The W3C PROV
ontology~\cite{lebo2013provo} defines the standard provenance data model;
SHOWME.how relies on SWMS-native provenance records rather than requiring a
dedicated provenance store.

\paragraph{Similar problems, different approaches.}
Pegasus~\cite{deelman2015pegasus} focuses on executing large-scale workflows
across heterogeneous distributed infrastructure, handling job scheduling, data
movement, and fault tolerance at scale. It treats each computational unit as a black box and makes no prescription about
how units should be packaged or what format data should take at their boundaries,
assuming these have already been decided. SHOWME.how addresses precisely those
questions through the Isolation and Interoperation pillars, and relies on existing
SWMSs for the scheduling and fault-tolerance concerns that are Pegasus's primary focus.
The Galaxy Project~\cite{galaxycommunity2024galaxy} enforces reproducibility through a
managed tool ecosystem, primarily for bioinformatics. Reproducibility is
achieved by requiring each tool to be registered in Galaxy's ToolShed with a
descriptor specifying its software dependencies and input/output parameters in
Galaxy's format, coupling reproducibility to Galaxy's own ecosystem.
SHOWME.how requires no tool registration or descriptor; isolation and
interoperation are applied at unit boundaries without modifying unit code.
AiiDA~\cite{huber2020aiida} provides graph-database provenance tracking and
automated workflow execution, originally designed for materials science and now
spanning multiple research domains. Its provenance guarantees are stronger than
SWMS-native reporting: all computational steps and data dependencies are
captured in a queryable graph database. The tradeoff is infrastructure
overhead: AiiDA requires a dedicated message broker (RabbitMQ), a running
database instance, and Python plugin wrappers for each code in the workflow.
SHOWME.how accepts weaker provenance in exchange: its records are SWMS-native
and require no additional infrastructure, but lack the queryable graph
structure and fine-grained dependency capture that AiiDA provides.

ColonyOS~\cite{kristiansson2025colonyos} is an open-source meta-OS targeting
IoT-to-HPC computing continuums for AI and machine-learning workflows. Its
central design constraint is that compute nodes may be geographically
dispersed, behind firewalls, or intermittently available; all coordination
therefore flows through a centralised Colonies server that executors poll over
HTTP rather than through direct connections. Executors are language-agnostic
microservices (SDKs exist for Python, Julia, Rust, Go, and others) that
exchange data via a meta-filesystem layer backed by S3 or IPFS. The three
concerns that result (per-executor isolation, language-agnostic exchange, and
broker-based orchestration) converge with the three pillars of SHOWME.how,
arrived at independently and for a different user community. The practical
difference is in the orchestration layer. ColonyOS requires a dedicated
Colonies server and database as a persistent broker; SHOWME.how relies on
SWMSs already established in scientific practice, with file or HTTP exchange
at unit boundaries and no additional broker infrastructure.

\section{Conclusion and Outlook}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% =============================================================================

This paper made two contributions. We introduced the \emph{computational lab}, a
goal-oriented sociotechnical system of computational units, data, and human
collaborators, a framing that, to our knowledge, is novel in the research software
engineering literature. It treats heterogeneity across data, methods, compute, and
people as the dimension to be managed, with reproducibility following as a
consequence rather than standing as the sole goal. We then presented
SHOWME.how, an approach resting on three complementary pillars, Isolation,
Interoperation, and Orchestration, that together let these heterogeneous parts be
combined without forcing any collaborator to abandon their own language, operating
system, or preferred tools.

The resulting labs are agnostic to the language, data format, and hardware of any
individual unit; modular and reusable across labs; portable from laptop to HPC and
cloud with no code changes to the units themselves; and free of the
wrapper-maintenance burden that has historically tied heterogeneous workflows to
stale interfaces. We grounded the approach in three use cases spanning biomedical
engineering, geophysical mass flow, and high-dimensional uncertainty
quantification, between them exercising all three recurring workflow patterns.
This is a concept paper, and the evidence we report is observational rather than a
quantitative evaluation against the robustness rubric of
Section~\ref{sec:robustness}.

Several directions remain open. The robustness rubric invites quantitative
criteria for scoring the robustness and heterogeneity coverage of a lab, which we
have not yet measured systematically. The tooling could be extended to better
support emerging infrastructure such as heterogeneous accelerators and federated
compute. It could also extend to streaming and digital-twin labs in which a model
receives continuous live input and updates online, a setting that sits outside the
batch directed-acyclic-graph paradigm of the workflow managers used here and would
draw on the reactive and streaming managers catalogued by the Workflows Community
Initiative~\cite{suter2025terminology}. Finally, a SHOWME.how lab already makes
explicit the unit contracts
that portable workflow descriptions require, so describing labs in CWL and
packaging them as RO-Crates~\cite{soilandreyes2022rocrate} for sharing through
WorkflowHub~\cite{goble2021workflowhub} is a natural next step.
