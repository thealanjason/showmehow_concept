\abstract{%
  Scientific discovery and engineering problem solving rely on computational
  methods implemented in diverse software ecosystems and executed on heterogeneous
  hardware platforms by collaborators with complementary skills and toolsets.
  We introduce the \emph{computational lab}: a goal-oriented sociotechnical system
  of \emph{computational units}, \emph{data}, and \emph{human collaborators}.
  Managing heterogeneity across all three makes assembling and sustaining such a lab
  non-trivial from the outset. Without a principled approach, the lab grows
  increasingly fragile as its components evolve independently.
  We present SHOWME.how, an approach built on three complementary pillars:
  \emph{Isolation}, \emph{Interoperation}, and \emph{Orchestration}.
  Computational units are isolated with their dependencies in a self-contained
  environment; they interoperate through language-agnostic data formats and
  transfer protocols; and data movement, scheduling, and resource management
  are orchestrated using workflow management systems.
  Together, the pillars ensure that labs tolerate updates to individual units,
  handle variability across languages and platforms, and accommodate new units
  without modifying existing ones, allowing them to sustainably grow and scale.
  We demonstrate the approach in three use cases: Bayesian parameter calibration
  in blood hemolysis and geophysical mass flow simulation, and benchmarking of
  high-dimensional Gaussian process emulators.%
}
\keywords{scientific workflows, heterogeneity, reproducibility, FAIR, computational labs,
          high-throughput computing, inverse problems, benchmarking}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% =============================================================================

\begin{itemize}
  \item Modern research is highly computational and spans large data, complex
        infrastructure, accelerators, and cross-disciplinary
        collaboration~\cite{cohenboulakia2017workflows,suter2025terminology}.
  \item FAIR principles guide management of data, software, and
        workflows~\cite{wilkinson2016fair,barker2022fair4rs,wilkinson2025fairworkflows,goble2020fairworkflows},
        but translating them into working infrastructure that handles real-world
        heterogeneity is non-trivial.
  \item Heterogeneity (data, methods, infrastructure, people) is the central
        unmanaged dimension. We introduce the \emph{computational lab}
        (Section~\ref{sec:complab}) and SHOWME.how (Section~\ref{sec:approach}),
        structured around three pillars: Isolation, Interoperation, Orchestration.
\end{itemize}


% =============================================================================
\section{The Computational Lab}
\label{sec:complab}
% =============================================================================

% AC===========================================================================
We define a \emph{computational lab} as a goal-oriented sociotechnical system
whose purpose is to enable scientific discovery and to address complex
engineering challenges through computational means. Typical goals range from answering
research questions, developing new models and algorithms, and understanding
complex natural phenomena, to solving inverse problems, performing
optimisation, and benchmarking competing methods for application-driven tasks.

The computational lab is to computation-driven research what the wet lab is to
experimental science. In both settings, work proceeds through a continuous
cycle of hypothesis, experiment, observation, analysis, and conclusion, applied
by scientists pursuing discovery and by engineers solving problems.
In practice, wet labs and computational labs are not disjoint. Contemporary
wet labs increasingly rely on computational labs for data acquisition,
processing, analysis, and modelling.

A computational lab is composed of three classes of components: computational
units, data, and human collaborators. A \emph{computational unit} couples a
\emph{model} or \emph{algorithm}, in its concrete implementation, with the
\emph{compute environment} in which it runs. The compute environment has two
layers. The software ecosystem covers the programming language and operating
system required by the implementation. The hardware platform covers the
physical resources on which the computational unit executes. \emph{Data} comprises the inputs
consumed by computational units, the outputs they produce, and the intermediate
artefacts exchanged between them. \emph{Human collaborators} are the
researchers, engineers, and domain experts who design and operate the lab, and
interpret its results. In principle this role could also be filled by AI agents, but we
restrict our attention to human collaborators in this work.

A central challenge in the operation of a computational lab is the management
of its heterogeneity. Each component is heterogeneous along its own axis.
Data appears in many formats and structures, from geometries, boundary
conditions, and observations as inputs, to quantities of interest,
visualisations, and reports as outputs. These data may reside on local disks,
in cloud object storage, or in community data repositories.
Models and algorithms span physics-based simulators, data-driven surrogates,
and frameworks for optimisation, inference, and analysis, each typically tied
to a particular language. Compute environments vary in both software ecosystem and hardware platform.
The software ecosystem ranges across programming languages such as Python,
Julia, R, C++, Fortran, and MATLAB, among others, and operating systems such as Linux,
macOS, and Windows. The hardware platform ranges across local workstations,
HPC clusters, and cloud, with accelerators such as GPUs alongside CPUs. This diversity is not incidental but a
direct consequence of the human collaborators themselves: domain specialists,
methods experts, data scientists, and research software engineers each bring
different skills, training, and preferred toolsets to the lab. A computational lab must therefore accommodate the tools and environments its
collaborators bring, rather than impose a single one.

The notion of a computational lab is distinct from several related terms. A
\emph{scientific workflow} captures the pipeline of computational steps but
treats neither heterogeneity nor human collaborators as first-class concerns.
A \emph{digital laboratory} (or \emph{e-lab}) usually denotes a domain-specific
platform built around LIMS or ELN systems for experimental data management.
The term \emph{computational laboratory} appears occasionally in the literature
but is used colloquially, without a formal definition.

Figure~\ref{fig:heterogeneous} illustrates these components in a representative
computational lab: the Bayesian parameter calibration of a shallow mass flow
model used in geophysical hazard simulation.
This inverse problem is solved by chaining a forward simulator, a surrogate
emulator, and a calibration step, each a separate computational unit developed and
operated by collaborators with complementary expertise. How these components are made
to work together is the subject of Section~\ref{sec:approach}.

\subsection{Recurring Patterns}
\label{sec:patterns}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------

% AC===========================================================================
Three patterns recur across computational labs in science and engineering
(Figure~\ref{fig:lab_patterns}); we introduce them here as they are
referenced throughout the paper.

\paragraph{Pattern A: Queue-Based Data Pipeline}
A \texttt{Generator} produces a large set of inputs (parameter sweeps,
different initial conditions, or sampled configurations); each generated
input is independently dispatched to a \texttt{Method} (a model or algorithm).
The \texttt{Method} processes each input and produces an output; the
outputs are collected by a \texttt{Collector} and aggregated into a
dataset for downstream use.
Common instances include training a surrogate emulator from a physics-based
simulator using a space-filling sampling scheme (see Figure~\ref{fig:heterogeneous}),
forward uncertainty propagation via Monte Carlo sampling where output statistics
are derived from the ensemble, and Sobol sensitivity analysis where the full
(input, output) dataset is processed to compute variance-based sensitivity indices.

\paragraph{Pattern B: Fan-Out/Fan-In}
A \texttt{Generator} produces a shared input that is dispatched to multiple
\texttt{Method} implementations; each produces an output, and the outputs
are gathered by a \texttt{Collector} for comparison.
Herein, the \texttt{Method} implementations are of the same model or algorithm,
differing in their mathematical formulation, programming language, or both.
Common instances include method benchmarking, where competing implementations
are evaluated against a shared input; ensemble runs with different random seeds,
whose outputs are aggregated to characterise variability or assess convergence;
and inference diagnostics, where shared inference results are fanned out to
complementary analyses and gathered into a report.

\paragraph{Pattern C: Model-Based $X$ in an Online Loop}
A \texttt{Model} and an \texttt{Algorithm} are coupled at run time as a
client--server pair; the \texttt{Algorithm} drives the \texttt{Model}
iteratively, requiring both to run simultaneously.
Common instances include inverse uncertainty quantification, such as parameter
calibration, model selection, and data selection, and optimisation, where
the model is queried iteratively to infer properties or search for optimal
inputs, and data assimilation, where the model state is updated sequentially
as new observations arrive.

In all three patterns, a computational unit may itself house tightly coupled
internal components, such as an MPI-parallel multi-physics solver or a
co-simulation.

\subsection{Robustness}
\label{sec:robustness_def}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------

% AC===========================================================================
Productive science and engineering requires that methods be reusable and
that new work can build on existing ones; to satisfy this, a computational
lab must keep running correctly as its data, methods, dependencies,
collaborators, and infrastructure change. Drawing on the general engineering
sense of the word, we say that a computational lab is \emph{robust} if it
satisfies four properties:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Tolerate perturbations}: remain functional when individual
        computational units are updated or replaced.
  \item \textbf{Handle variability}: remain effective across diverse operating
        systems, programming languages, hardware platforms, and data formats.
  \item \textbf{Withstand adverse conditions}: remain operational under
        infrastructure migration and collaborator changes, and recoverable on
        interruption of a long-running computation.
  \item \textbf{Support modular extensibility}: accommodate new computational
        units and new collaborators added after the lab is first assembled,
        without breaking existing ones.
\end{enumerate}

The fourth is added because computational labs are by design constantly
evolving: they grow as new scientific questions are asked and new methods
become available. A robustness framing that does not account for this growth
is insufficient for the research setting.

\section{Status Quo and Challenges}
\label{sec:statusquo}
% =============================================================================

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Lack of unit-level reproducibility}: missing/incomplete environment
        specs prevent rebuilding; workflow reproducibility also needs RNG seeds, OS
        behaviour, and parallel ordering to be pinned.
  \item \textbf{Data feedforward approach}: manual file transfer between
        collaborators preserves environments but loses versioning, traceability, and
        provenance; physically infeasible for tight loops like Bayesian calibration
        that need thousands of model queries.
  \item \textbf{Software wrapper approach}: unified interfaces enable in-memory
        sharing but go stale, lock the lab to old versions, and block adoption of
        newly published methods in other languages.
  \item \textbf{Language-tied data formats} (\texttt{.pkl}, \texttt{.npy},
        \texttt{.jld2}): convenient within one ecosystem, but force collaborators to
        convert data or switch languages, undermining heterogeneity. Concretely,
        \texttt{.jld2} is HDF5-based but Julia type metadata is lost when read in
        Python via \texttt{h5py} --- only raw array data is recoverable.
  \item What is missing is not more tools, but an approach that makes
        computational labs robust by design.
\end{itemize}

\section{SHOWME.how}
\label{sec:approach}
% =============================================================================

\begin{itemize}
  \item Three complementary principles --- Isolation, Interoperation, Orchestration
        (Figure~\ref{fig:three_pillars}); use cases (Section~\ref{sec:usecases})
        instantiate the patterns from Section~\ref{sec:complab}.
  \item Collaborators are beneficiaries of the three pillars rather than a
        separate technical pillar themselves: the pillars let each collaborator
        keep their preferred language, operating system, and toolset.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Isolation}
\label{sec:isolation}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------

\begin{itemize}
  \item Encapsulates each computational unit with its full dependencies in a
        self-contained, portable environment.
  \item \textbf{conda/mamba}~\cite{conda,mamba}: cross-platform, language-agnostic
        package managers; \texttt{environment.yml} alone is not reproducible.
  \item \textbf{conda-lock files} pin every package per (OS $\times$ architecture):
        \texttt{linux-64}, \texttt{linux-aarch64}, \texttt{osx-64}, \texttt{osx-arm64},
        \texttt{win-64}; sufficient for numerically equivalent reproducibility.
  \item Bit-identical reproducibility additionally requires controlling host system
        libraries (\texttt{glibc}), BLAS variants/threading, and floating-point
        nondeterminism in parallel reductions.
  \item \textbf{Docker/Apptainer}~\cite{merkel2014docker,kurtzer2017singularity,apptainer}:
        package the OS layer; Docker on Windows/macOS runs in a Linux VM, so host-side
        IPC primitives (Unix sockets, named pipes, \texttt{/dev/shm}) are not
        reachable.
  \item HPC clusters typically forbid Docker; pipeline:
        \texttt{Dockerfile} $\to$ Docker image (laptop/cloud) $\to$ Apptainer
        \texttt{.sif} (HPC).
  \item Multi-architecture images (\texttt{linux/amd64} + \texttt{linux/arm64})
        built via \texttt{docker buildx} under a single manifest.
  \item \textbf{Containerising conda environments}: wrap locked conda env in a
        container to remove host leakage. Snakemake's \texttt{--containerize}
        bundles all workflow envs into one image~\cite{molder2021snakemake};
        Nextflow's Wave provisions per-process images on
        demand~\cite{ditommaso2017nextflow}.
  \item Trade-off: bundled image (single artefact, shared OS layer) vs.\ per-unit
        images (true unit-level isolation, more artefacts). Per-unit aligns with
        SHOWME.how.
  \item \textbf{CI automation}: matrix builds regenerate lock files on
        \texttt{environment.yml} changes and publish multi-arch images on Dockerfile
        changes, shifting maintenance from collaborators to a single pipeline.
\end{itemize}

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Interoperation}
\label{sec:interoperation}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------

\begin{itemize}
  \item Once units are isolated, data must flow between them; maps onto the
        Interoperability dimension of FAIR~\cite{wilkinson2016fair}.
  \item Two sub-concerns: \emph{data interchange} (format) and \emph{data exchange}
        (protocol).
  \item \textbf{Interchange formats}: CSV/JSON are human-readable but poor for
        numerics; language-specific binaries (\texttt{.npy}, \texttt{.pkl},
        \texttt{.jld2}) are not portable. Use:
        \begin{itemize}
          \item \textbf{HDF5}/netCDF for hierarchical, structured
                data~\cite{hdf5}.
          \item \textbf{Zarr} for multi-dimensional array data, with HTTP/S3
                optimisation~\cite{moore2023zarr}.
        \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Exchange protocols}: start with file-system exchange; use in-process
        IPC via \texttt{/tmp} only when throughput demands it (and not with Docker
        on non-Linux).
  \item \textbf{UMBridge}~\cite{seelinger2023umbridge}: cross-language client--server
        coupling over JSON/HTTP, enabling online model--UQ coupling without manual
        transfer. JSON is a throughput bottleneck for large arrays; no binary wire
        format yet.
  \item Decision heuristic: file-system $\to$ in-process IPC $\to$ HTTP/UMBridge or
        object storage (S3) for cross-infrastructure or high-throughput coupling.
\end{itemize}

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Orchestration}
\label{sec:orchestration}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------

\begin{itemize}
  \item Automates coordination as labs grow: data movement, dependency-based
        scheduling, checkpointing, resource monitoring, and laptop $\to$ HPC $\to$
        cloud portability without modification.
  \item We use \textbf{Nextflow}~\cite{ditommaso2017nextflow} and
        \textbf{Snakemake}~\cite{molder2021snakemake} as representative examples;
        SHOWME.how is not tied to any manager. Broader landscape in the Workflows
        Community Initiative~\cite{suter2025terminology}.
  \item Both share the contract: each step declares inputs, outputs, script, and
        compute environment (conda or container) --- maps directly onto isolation.
  \item \textbf{Nextflow}: dataflow paradigm, highly structured; UNIX-only (WSL on
        Windows); extensions require Groovy/JVM; no native service-process support
        (worked around with file-based sentinels for UMBridge servers).
  \item \textbf{Snakemake}: file-based paradigm, Python extension --- cross-platform
        and familiar to scientists; well-positioned for the full orchestration ideal.
        Docker is second-class (transparently swapped for Apptainer).
\end{itemize}

\section{Use Cases}
\label{sec:usecases}
% =============================================================================

\begin{itemize}
  \item Applied across several domains (Table~\ref{tab:usecases}); not exhaustive of
        the approach's scope.
  \item \textbf{Bayesian calibration --- blood hemolysis}: pattern~(3); domain
        expert (macOS, Python), UQ-methods expert (Windows, R+Python), and RSE
        (Linux, multi-language) collaborating without changing tools; the RSE
        ported the domain expert's Python models to Julia for a 10--100$\times$
        speedup over the original NumPy baseline. Observational evidence: ported
        laptop~$\to$~HPC with only manager-profile changes (scheduler +
        Docker~$\to$~Apptainer), no unit-code changes; runs end-to-end on Linux,
        macOS, Windows with the same isolated units (Python/Julia via HDF5);
        diagnostic units added post-hoc without touching existing ones. conda-lock
        pinning and per-thread RNG seeding planned, not yet demonstrated.
        Repository: \url{https://github.com/nicodirkes/bpc_hemolysis}.
  \item \textbf{Bayesian calibration --- mass flow simulation}: patterns~(1)+(3);
        expensive HPC simulator $\to$ surrogate training $\to$ UMBridge-coupled UQ
        loop; three isolated environments; bulk file exchange + live HTTP coupling;
        lab in development.
        Repository: \url{https://github.com/thealanjason/bpc_massflow}.
  \item \textbf{Benchmarking of GP emulators for high-dimensional UQ}: pattern~(2);
        multiple GP implementations across languages, including newly published
        methods; language-agnostic shared benchmark data; parallel execution with
        incremental checkpointing; lab in development.
        Repository: \url{https://github.com/gary8564/gpe-bench}.
\end{itemize}

\section{Robustness}
\label{sec:robustness}
% =============================================================================

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Robustness checklist}, with each criterion answering a status-quo
        failure mode (Section~\ref{sec:statusquo}):
        \begin{itemize}
          \item \emph{Perturbations} (vs.\ missing env specs): per-OS/per-arch lock
                files for every unit; container-wrapped locked env where
                bit-identical reproducibility is required; explicit RNG seeding
                per-thread/rank and per-library (a single global seed is
                insufficient under OpenMP/MPI; NumPy legacy vs.\ \texttt{Generator},
                Julia \texttt{Random}/\texttt{MersenneTwister}, R \texttt{set.seed},
                and third-party libs must each be seeded);
                workflow-manager checkpointing enabled.
          \item \emph{Variability} (vs.\ language-tied formats): workflow runs on
                $>1$ host OS with same locks/images; no language-tied serialisation
                on unit boundaries; cross-infrastructure coupling via language-/
                host-agnostic protocol (HTTP via UMBridge, S3).
          \item \emph{Adverse conditions} (vs.\ wrappers and feedforward): single
                Dockerfile drives both Docker (laptop) and Apptainer (HPC) images
                with no unit-code changes; multi-arch manifests where needed; no
                wrapper code bridges languages in shared address space; lock/image
                generation automated in CI.
          \item \emph{Modular extensibility} (vs.\ wrapper lock-in): each unit
                declares inputs/outputs/script/env as a self-contained contract;
                adding a new unit requires no edits or revalidation of existing
                units.
        \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Honest assessment}: no quantitative measurements (glue-code lines,
        onboarding time, build times, cross-OS numerical equivalence, restart
        overhead, post-hoc additions without regression) collected systematically;
        quantitative evaluation against the checklist remains future work.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
% =============================================================================

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{FAIR}~\cite{wilkinson2016fair,barker2022fair4rs,goble2020fairworkflows,wilkinson2025fairworkflows}:
        normative framework; SHOWME.how is a concrete implementation strategy for
        Interoperability and Reusability dimensions.
  \item \textbf{Scientific workflow systems}: Nextflow~\cite{ditommaso2017nextflow},
        Snakemake~\cite{molder2021snakemake}; broader surveys by Suter et
        al.~\cite{suter2025terminology}, Leser et al.~\cite{leser2026workflowsystems};
        reproducibility evaluation by Dierckx et al.~\cite{dierckx2022evaluation}.
  \item \textbf{Reproducibility}: Cohen-Boulakia et al.~\cite{cohenboulakia2017workflows}
        focus on reproducibility in life-science workflows; SHOWME.how generalises
        to heterogeneity management with reproducibility as a consequence
        (Table~\ref{tab:cohenboulakia}).
  \item \textbf{UMBridge}~\cite{seelinger2023umbridge}: key enabling tool for
        Interoperation; language-agnostic HTTP client--server for model--UQ
        coupling without wrappers.
  \item \textbf{Workflow standards/packaging}:
        CWL~\cite{amstutz2016cwl} (vendor-neutral portable workflow standard),
        WorkflowHub~\cite{goble2021workflowhub} (community registry),
        RO-Crate Workflow profile~\cite{soilandreyes2022rocrate} (FAIR packaging);
        complementary to SHOWME.how, which uses managers directly.
  \item \textbf{HPC-native/reproducible package management}:
        Spack~\cite{gamblin2015spack},
        Nix/Guix~\cite{courtes2015guix} (cryptographic reproducibility); stricter
        than conda-lock; compatible with SHOWME.how, which uses conda-lock as a
        pragmatic default.
  \item \textbf{Columnar formats}: Apache Arrow/Parquet~\cite{apachearrow} for
        zero-copy, language-agnostic tabular data --- complements HDF5/Zarr for
        tabular workflows.
  \item \textbf{Orchestration and provenance}: AiiDA~\cite{huber2020aiida}
        (graph-database provenance), Pegasus~\cite{deelman2015pegasus} (large-scale
        HPC/cloud planning), PROV-O~\cite{lebo2013provo} (W3C provenance
        ontology); future direction for SHOWME.how.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% =============================================================================

\begin{itemize}
  \item We contribute both an \textbf{approach} (SHOWME.how: Isolation,
        Interoperation, Orchestration) and a \textbf{concept} (the
        \emph{computational lab}), positioning heterogeneity --- across data,
        methods, compute, and people --- rather than reproducibility as the
        primary managed dimension; novel framing in the RSE literature.
  \item Resulting labs are language-/format-/hardware-agnostic per unit; modular,
        reusable, and portable laptop~$\to$~HPC~$\to$~cloud without unit-code
        changes or wrapper-maintenance burden.
  \item Demonstrated across all three generic patterns in biomedical engineering,
        geophysical mass flow, and high-dimensional UQ methods development.
  \item \textbf{Future work}: formalising robustness/heterogeneity-coverage criteria;
        supporting heterogeneous accelerators and federated environments; extending
        to streaming/digital-twin labs outside the batch-DAG paradigm;
        operationalising collaborator roles (RSE, domain expert, methods
        expert) as first-class interfaces.
\end{itemize}
